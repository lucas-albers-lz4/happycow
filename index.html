Acoustic Characterization and Optimal Synthesis of a Deployed Bovine Vocalization Asset for a Static Web Application
The Happy Cow Research Collective1,2 · Department of Applied Mooing1 · BigSky Audio Institute2
(Corresponding author: the cow. Do not email the cow.)
Abstract. We present a comprehensive investigation into the procurement of an authentic Bos taurus vocalization (hereafter, "the moo") for the Happy Cow web application — a static, zero-backend happy hour directory serving Bozeman, Montana. The initial deployed asset, herein designated Moo₀, was found upon expert audition to exhibit "a spectral character inconsistent with the local bovine population" (i.e., it sounded fake). Through Fourier analysis (Equation 1), harmonic verification, spectral flatness metrics, and a Montana-grade expert taste test, we identified, extracted, filtered, and normalized a genuine bellow. We further report the surprising result that the incumbent asset, despite possessing a plausible 189 Hz fundamental, was nevertheless judged "not a real cow" by a panel of one (1) Montanan. All results are reproducible via a single curl command and 1.6 seconds of ffmpeg.
1. Introduction
Cows are abundant in Montana (population of B. taurus ≈ 2.9M; human population ≈ 1.1M [1]). The application in question, Happy Cow, is a single-page static site served from GitHub Pages at zero marginal cost, featuring 30 procedurally-rotated cow illustrations and a cow-of-the-day selector. When the user taps the cow, the site plays an audio asset via the Web Audio HTMLAudioElement API. The requirement — "make it sound like a cow" — was stated by a stakeholder with direct, first-hand, multi-year exposure to actual cows. This is the strictest known acceptance criterion in the literature.
2. The Failure of Moo₀
The incumbent asset, Moo₀ (15,091 bytes, 1.15 s, MPEG ADTS layer III v2, 96 kbps, 22.05 kHz mono), was subjected to spectral analysis. Its dominant peak was found at 189.3 Hz (Table 1, row 1) with a spectral flatness suggesting synthetic origin. Critically, a double-blind taste test — conducted at an elevation of 4,800 ft with a single participant who has personally known cows by name — returned a verdict of "that is not what a cow sounds like, and we have a lot of them here." This falsifies the hypothesis that "low frequency ≈ moo." We conclude that Moo₀ was a tone with cow-adjacent pitch but zero cow-adjacent soul.
3. Methodology
3.1 Candidate corpus. Candidate vocalizations were located via the Wikimedia Commons API (namespaces ns=6, search term "cow moo filetype:audio") and a CC0 sound bank. Three candidates of interest were retained: Single Cow Moo (CC BY-SA 4.0, 53,861 B), Mudchute cow 1 (CC BY-SA 3.0, 25,975 B), and BSB cow moos (CC0, 574,208 B, 14.33 s).
3.2 Spectral characterization. Each candidate was decoded to 16 kHz mono PCM and analyzed via the Short-Time Fourier Transform with Hann windowing (64 ms), from which the dominant in-band (60–600 Hz) component was extracted per 250 ms hop.
X[k] = Σn=0N−1 w[n] · x[n] · e−j2πkn/N   (1)
3.3 Harmonic verification. The winning candidate was verified voiced via spectral flatness γ = exp(⟨ln S⟩) / ⟨S⟩ = 0.0114 ≪ 0.1, confirming harmonic-rich phonation (Table 1).
3.4 Extraction. The loudest window (t = 6.3–8.1 s, RMS 0.32) was cut with input-side seeking (-ss before -i; placing it after was found to produce silence, a lesson we share at no charge), high-passed at 60 Hz, low-passed at 900 Hz, gained ×1.6, and fade-out applied over the final 300 ms.

4. Results and Discussion
The deployed asset Moo₁ (37,021 bytes, 1.80 s, 160 kbps) exhibits a fundamental of 270 Hz with strong first harmonic, spectral flatness 0.0114, mean level −9.1 dB, and peak 0.0 dB. At the time of writing, it has been confirmed by the stakeholder as "sounding like a cow," a result we consider the definitive acceptance signal, exceeding all published benchmarks in the domain. We note the moo is played with stochastic playback rate (0.75–1.45×) and gain (0.55–1.0), yielding a herd-like distribution of vocalizations from a single asset — a form of data augmentation that, to our knowledge, has not been previously reported for bovids.
5. Conclusion
We replaced a 189 Hz synthetic tone with a 270 Hz genuine bellow at a total cost of zero dollars and one (1) deeply satisfying ffmpeg pipeline. This work is of no use to anyone building an actual happy hour directory, which is exactly why it is documented here in excruciating detail instead of, say, the feature set, the data pipeline, or how to contribute.
